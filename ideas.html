Linework · What's next

The pedals still to come

Every Linework pedal keeps its sound analog. These are effects where analog circuits are the whole point, and where linking to Switchyard adds something a normal pedal can't do.

Nothing here is designed yet. Features, names and order may change.

In planning

Next in the series

Tap Changer

Clean boost

More volume with no change in tone, for solos or for pushing an amp harder.

Why analog
A simple, quiet analog stage is about as transparent as a pedal gets.
Linked
Switches on by itself for the solo and off again after it.
The name
A tap changer is the part of a power transformer that steps the voltage up a notch.

Three Phase

Phaser

The classic swirl, from a slow sweep to a fast, watery wobble.

Why analog
The sound comes from a chain of analog phase-shift stages, the same idea as the 1970s originals.
Linked
Speed locked to each song's tempo from the setlist. Analog phasers normally can't do that.
The name
Power grids run on three phases, each shifted from the others.

Rolling Blackout

Tremolo

Rhythmic volume pulsing, from a gentle throb to a hard chop, like vintage amps.

Why analog
A light and a light-sensitive cell give the soft, rounded pulse of old amp tremolo.
Linked
Speed synced to the song's tempo, part by part.
The name
A rolling blackout switches the power off and on again, in turn.

Double Circuit

Chorus

Shimmering chorus and wobbling vibrato that make one guitar sound like two.

Why analog
"Bucket brigade" delay chips give the warm, slightly dark sound players look for in chorus.
Linked
Chorus in the verse, vibrato in the bridge, with speeds synced to tempo.
The name
A double-circuit power line carries two circuits side by side on the same towers.

Further out

More ideas

Overdrive

Smooth, amp-like breakup that cleans up when you pick lightly. A warmer, gentler partner for Flashover.

Why analog
Diode clipping inside an op-amp's feedback loop reacts to every change in picking strength.
Linked
Light gain for the verse, more for the chorus, set for each part of the song.

Fuzz

A transistor fuzz: thick, woolly and loud, and it cleans up when you roll back the guitar's volume.

Why analog
Fuzz is the effect players most insist on analog for: the transistors react to the guitar itself.
Linked
The transistor's bias can be set digitally, so one song part gets smooth fuzz and the next a sputtering, starved one.

Octave fuzz

Fuzz with a ringing octave up above the note, best on lead lines played high on the neck.

Why analog
The octave comes from a simple rectifier circuit whose quirks are hard to copy digitally.
Linked
On for one riff, off for the rest of the song, with no tap dancing.

Compressor

Evens out your playing and adds sustain, from a light touch on chords to heavy squash on clean leads.

Why analog
Analog gain control squeezes smoothly and quietly, with no digital artifacts on the attack.
Linked
Sustain and attack set for each part, so chords and leads each get their own setting.

Envelope filter

An auto-wah that sweeps with how hard you pick: funky, vocal and very touch sensitive.

Why analog
Analog filters follow the pick instantly and sweep with no stepping.
Linked
Sensitivity and range set for each song, matched to how hard that song is played.

What would you want next?

Every one of these would work on its own as a normal pedal, and link to Switchyard like Flashover does. Delay and reverb aren't on this list: they're better done digitally, and we're planning them for a version of Switchyard with built-in effects.
